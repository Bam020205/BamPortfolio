      Paul then describes what love looks like in practice. Love is patient and kind. It does not envy, boast, or insist on its own way. It rejoices in truth, bears and endures. These are not merely admirable traits; they show the heart and character of Jesus.

      So when I read, “Love is patient,” I can ask: Is Jesus patient with people through the way I respond to them? When I read, “Love is kind,” I can ask whether my words and choices reflect his kindness. As I learn to follow Jesus, I can pray that Abram will become more patient, more kind, and less focused on himself—not to make Abram look good, but so that Jesus’ character becomes visible in Abram’s life.

      Love is not just a feeling or a label. It is the way I choose to use my will, words, abilities, and spiritual gifts for the good of others.

      3. Love remains; our gifts are partial and temporary

      1 Corinthians 13:8–12

      Paul says that prophecies, tongues, and knowledge will pass away, while love never fails. He is not saying that spiritual gifts are worthless. He is reminding the Corinthians that even genuine gifts provide only a partial understanding of God’s truth. A prophecy may reveal something God chooses to disclose, but it does not contain the fullness of God’s knowledge.

      The Corinthians were impressed by spiritual gifts, but Paul directs them toward what endures. The strongest sign of maturity is not how impressive my gift appears, but whether I use it with love.

      A life offered to God

      Paul’s message applies not only to spiritual gifts but to everything God has placed in our hands: our time, education, talents, relationships, responsibilities, and influence. The goal is not simply to accomplish more. It is to use what we have in a way that makes God’s goodness known.

      I want to become an instrument through which God serves others and is glorified. That means surrendering my motives as well as my actions. I can ask God to show me where I am seeking recognition, holding on to pride, or serving only when it benefits me—and to teach me to act with the patient, humble love of Jesus.

      I may pray to live as a “living martyr”: not by seeking harm or treating suffering as a goal, but by surrendering my self-centered desires and choosing faithfulness to God in ordinary life. That can mean giving up the need to be praised, choosing forgiveness when resentment feels easier, serving when no one notices, and doing what is right even when it costs me comfort or status. The sacrifice is not an attempt to earn God’s love. It is a response to the love Jesus has already shown.

      May my gifts never become a way to glorify myself. May my words, choices, and service reflect the love of Jesus, so that his name—not mine—is the name that is glorified.

### When Gifts Become Love

**Scripture: 1 Corinthians 13**

Paul wrote to the church in Corinth around AD 53–55, a community known for its wealth, social divisions, and moral struggles. In the chapters around 1 Corinthians 13, he discusses spiritual gifts and how they should serve the church. But the Corinthians had started to treat impressive gifts as proof of spiritual maturity. Paul interrupts that discussion with a chapter about _agape_—self-giving love.

He is not asking the church to choose between love and spiritual gifts. Gifts matter. The question is what they are for. Paul’s answer is that gifts must be guided by love and used to serve others.
